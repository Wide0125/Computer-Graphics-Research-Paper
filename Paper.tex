\section{Introduction}
The world is $3$-dimensional. In contrast, visual perceptions of the world are $2$-dimensional, and yet, recognizable as of a $3$D space. Additionally, through this view, one can still perceive the depth of the image, retrieving some of the information lost in the process of flattening the view into $2$ dimensions. This phenomenon reveals the possibility of projecting a $3$D space onto a $2$D plane, allowing us to emulate the existence of a $3$D space behind a $2$D plane (much like looking through a window to look at the outside world; a detailed enough $2$D image on the window would be indistinguishable from a view of the outside).

Furthermore, this possibility is taken advantage of to form the foundations of modern computer graphics. When it comes to video games, computer generated imagery, or $3$D animation, computers must simulate all the physical processes of the real world that produce the light and resulting image that reaches your eyes. Although the entirety of the physical world cannot possibly be simulated within the constraints of modern computing capabilities, mathematical and physical abstractions can be made in order to simplify this process and produce near true to life results. These abstractions form the basis of $3$D Computer Graphics.

In modern computing, it is unnecessary to know the fundamental concepts and processes behind projecting a $3$D image; with the release of many software solutions and libraries that aid in the development of appropriate visuals, the modern day developer does not need to grasp the underlying mechanics behind $3$D Computer Graphics in order to produce satisfactory results. However, the benefits of learning the fundamental process behind this widespread field are essential for those who wish to become experts not tied down by any specific engine or library. Factors such as performance, scalability, and customizability justify the process of learning the fundamentals.\todo{Add real-world examples of mentioned advantages} Above all else, those who understand are better equipped to take advantage of and develop graphical toolkits. As such, it is imperative to understand the foundations that make up $3$D Computer Graphics for those who wish to become true masters of the field.

This paper sets out to document the process of learning the fundamentals of $3$D Computer Graphics, with real code examples mapping out the learning process. As such, it aims to serve as a useful guide that directs readers to explore the bare-bones fundamentals of $3$D Computer Graphics. However, it will not contribute anything of substantial originality to the field, and will be written from the perspective of a novice learner.

\section{3 Dimensional Space}
\subsection{Coordinates and Vectors}


In order to represent a $3$-dimensional space, we must first develop the tools used for defining a $3$D space.

A \textbf{cartesian coordinate system} is a system of numerically representing space with a set of coordinates. 
$n$-dimension spaces require $n$ sets of coordinates to represent. As such, a $3$-dimension cartesian coordinate system requires $3$ sets of coordinates, commonly referred to as $x$, $y$, and $z$.

A \textbf{point} is a single position in space, represented by a set of coordinates, and notated by $(C_{1} , C_{2}, C_{3}, \ldots, C_{n})$. A $3$D cartesian coordinate system places the center, or the origin point at $(0, 0, 0)$. Each dimension, represented through an axis, has a direction: traveling towards the direction of an axis is known as traveling positively, while traveling against the direction of an axis is known as traveling negatively, with the corresponding coordinates increasing or decreasing appropriately.\autocite{Eldridge2023}

\begin{figure}[htp]
    \centering
    \includesvg{Figure-1.svg}
    \caption{The point (1, 1, 1) in a 3D cartesian coordinate system}\label{figure:point111}
\end{figure}

The directions of each axis in relation to each other can be remembered using the right hand rule (Figure~\ref{figure:righthandrule}).
\begin{figure}[htp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[scale=0.2]{Right-hand rule.png}
    \caption{Right-hand rule}\label{figure:righthandrule}
\end{figure}
Axis configurations corresponding to the left hand also exist, however the right-handed system is more common.

\newpage

\textbf{Vector}s are the differences between points. Vectors can be used to represent many things, such as motion and direction. At its simplest, a vector can be intuitively thought of as a set of components that represent a certain magnitude of motion in a certain direction. It can also be thought of as the concept of a slope used in $2$D cartesian coordinate systems expanded for $n$-dimensions. Visually, it is a line connecting two points in space (Figure~\ref{figure:v000to111}).

\begin{figure}[htp]
    \centering
    \includesvg[scale=0.95]{Figure-3}
    \caption{A vector from point $(0, 0, 0)$ to $(1, 1, 1)$}\label{figure:v000to111}
\end{figure}
   
$n$-dimension vectors can be notated as a set of $n$ components:
\[
\vec{V} = 
\begin{bmatrix}
    V_{1} \\
    V_{2} \\
    V_{3} \\
    \vdots \\
    V_{n}
\end{bmatrix} \quad
\text{or} \quad
\vec{V} = \langle V_{1}, V_{2}, V_{3}, \ldots, V_{n} \rangle
\]
Much like the slope of a line in $2$ dimensions, vectors can be thought of as the ratio between each of the components’ deltas (differences in magnitude). For example, take the $3$D vector $\langle 1, 2, 3 \rangle$: when $x$ is increased by $1$, $y$ is increased by $2$, and $z$ is increased by $3$ (Figure~\ref{figure:vectorcomponents}).\autocite{BritannicaVector2004}

\begin{figure}[htp]
    \centering
    \includesvg[scale=0.95]{Figure-4.svg}
    \caption{The vector $\langle 1, 2, 3 \rangle$ broken down into its $x$, $y$, and $z$ components}\label{figure:vectorcomponents}
\end{figure}

Arithmetic operations can also be performed on vectors. Vector addition is done by summing together each respective component. For example, the resulting vector of adding vectors $\langle A_{x}, A_{y}, A_{z} \rangle$ and $\langle B_{x}, B_{y}, B_{z} \rangle$ together is vector $\langle A_{x} + B_{x}, A_{y} + B_{y}, A_{z} + B_{z} \rangle$. Visually, adding two vectors involves putting the beginning of a vector at the end of another (Figure~\ref{figure:vectorAddition}). Additionally, two types of vector multiplication exist: the \textbf{dot product} and the \textbf{cross product}. The dot product (denoted as $\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B}$) is used to find how similar two vectors are to each other, and results in a scalar\footnote{A scalar is a value that possesses only magnitude. It differs from a vector value in that vectors possess both magnitude and direction.} value representing similarity. A vector dot product with itself produces a scalar square of its magnitude. The cross product (denoted as $\vec{A} \times \vec{B}$) by contrast is used to find a vector that is the most different to the vectors it operates on. The cross product of two vectors results in a vector perpendicular to the plane created by the two vectors (Figure~\ref{v011&111crossproduct}). Formulas for the dot product (\ref{equation:dotproduct}) and the cross product (\ref{equation:crossproduct}) are listed below.

\begin{figure}[htp]
    \centering
    \includesvg{Vector_addition.svg}\caption{}\label{figure:vectorAddition}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htp]
    \centering
    \includesvg[scale=0.9]{Figure-9}
    \caption{The cross product of two vectors $\langle 0, 1, 1 \rangle$ and $\langle 1, 1, 1 \rangle$}\label{v011&111crossproduct}
\end{figure}

\begin{equation}\label{equation:dotproduct}
    \vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = A_{x}B_{x} + A_{y}B_{y} + A_{z}B_{z}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{equation:crossproduct}
    \begin{split}
        \vec{A} \times \vec{B} =
        \begin{bmatrix}
            A_{y}B_{z} - A_{z}B_{y} \\
            A_{z}B_{x} - A_{x}B_{z} \\
            A_{x}B_{y} - A_{y}B_{x}
        \end{bmatrix}
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

Notably, the dot product is nothing more than the sum of the products of each of the components; as such, the commutative and distributive properties of arithmetic multiplication also apply.

\[
\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = \vec{B} \cdot \vec{A}
\]
\[
(\vec{A} + \vec{B}) \cdot \vec{C} = \vec{A} \cdot \vec{C} + \vec{B} \cdot \vec{C}
\]

Scalar multiplication of vectors is also possible.

\[
t\vec{V} = \langle tV_{x}, tV_{y}, tV_{z} \rangle
\]

Vectors are useful in that they allow us to easily define movement or position differences in any direction.

A \textbf{line} is a collection of points arranged in a straight path, extending infinitely in opposing directions.\autocite{BritannicaLine2009} In $2$D, a line is most commonly defined as an equation conveying the relationship between the two coordinate variables $x$ and $y$ (\ref{equation:2dline}). Although this can be expanded to $3$ dimensions (\ref{equation:2dline}), a more convenient and commonly used definition utilizes vectors (\ref{equation:3dpara}). In this definition, every point $P$ along the line is defined by adding a multiple $t$ ($t \in \mathbb{R}$) of $\vec{D}$ to a point of origin $O$. Intuitively, it can be thought of as starting from an origin point $O$ and traveling $t$ amount in the direction of the vector component $\vec{D}$.

Line definitions allow us to calculate intersections involving objects traveling in a straight path.

\begin{equation}\label{equation:2dline}
    y = mx + b
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{equation:3dpara}
    \frac{x - x_{0}}{a} = \frac{y - y_{0}}{b} = \frac{z - z_{0}}{c}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{equation:3dvect}
    P = O + t\vec{D}
\end{equation}

Now that we have established a system of rules for representing space numerically, we can begin to lay the groundwork for projecting a $3$D space onto a $2$D plane.

\section{Ray Tracing}
\subsection{Viewports and Rays}
The process of projecting a $3$D view onto a $2$D plane is inspired by how vision and perspective works in the real world. Vision in the real world is essentially a $2$D image of a $3$D landscape.

A person is looking out onto a landscape and would like to save this view onto a canvas. An artist could simply paint the view, however this person lacks the skills required. Cleverly, they set up a net the same size as the canvas in a position that captures the desired view. Looking through a square hole of the net, the person averages out the color that they can see through that hole and paints the location corresponding to that hole on the canvas with that color. Repeating this step for every single hole results in an accurate sketch of the landscape.\autocite{Gambetta2021}

This metaphor is useful in that it can be applied to computers. Computers display images as a grid of square pixels, much like the square holes of the net used in the analogy.

A rectangle referred to as the \textbf{viewport} is placed in the world space, the equivalent to the square net. Then a camera is placed behind the viewport, the equivalent to the person's eyes (Figure-\ref{figure:viewport}).

\begin{figure}[htp]
    \centering
    \includesvg{Figure-5}
    \caption{A viewport of a 16:9 screen with its center located at $(0, 0, 1)$}\label{figure:viewport}
\end{figure}

In the net analogy, the colors that reach the person's eyes are certain wavelengths of photons that travel from an origin light source, bounce off reflective objects, and ultimately end up in their retinas. Although we could simulate photons traveling in all direction from a light source, that would be highly redundant as most of them would not end up hitting the camera. Instead, it is more efficient to trace in reverse: light rays traveling outwards from the camera and into any objects.

A \textbf{ray} is a collection of points arranged in a straight path extending infinitely towards a single direction. Whereas a line extends infinitely in two opposing directions from a point, rays only extend in a single direction.

In practice, this means that we compute shooting a light ray from the camera to a pixel's equivalent location on the viewport, and if it intersects with an object, color in that pixel on the screen with the color of the object. We repeat this for every pixel until every pixel on the screen is correctly colored in. This technique is known as \textbf{ray tracing}.

\begin{figure}[htp]
    \centering
    \includesvg{Figure-6}
    \caption{A vector from camera to pixel $(0, 0)$'s equivalent location on viewport}\label{figure:raytopixelonviewport}
\end{figure}

Now that a system for projecting a $3$D space onto a $2$D plane has been established, what remains is to implement said system.

Now that a method for obtaining the light ray has been determined, we can begin to define objects to display. For this early implementation, a sphere will be used as it is mathematically simple. A sphere is defined as a collection of points that are the same distance away from a single point. Every point $P$ along the surface of a sphere with center $C$ and radius $r$ can be defined as such:
\[
distance(P, C) = r^{2}
\]
The function $distance(x, y)$ takes in two points as input and outputs the distance between the two points.

All that's left is to determine whether the light ray for our pixel and any point on the sphere intersect; if so, color in that pixel.

By substituting the point on the surface of the sphere $P$ with the equation for our light ray, we can apply the discriminant to the resulting equation in order to determine if a solution $t$ such that the sphere and ray intersect exist.

\[
P = C_{p} + t\vec{D} (t \geq 0)
\]
\[
distance(P, C) = r^{2}
\]

\begin{align}
    distance(C_{p} + t\vec{D}, C) = r^{2} \\
    \{(C_{p} + t\vec{D}) - C\} \cdot \{(C_{p} + t\vec{D}) - C\} = r^{2}\label{equation:dotproductexpansion} \\
    \{t\vec{D} + (C_{p} - C)\} \cdot \{t\vec{D} + (C_{p} - C)\} = r^{2} \\
    t\vec{D} \cdot \{t\vec{D} + (C_{p} - C)\} + (C_{p} - C) \cdot \{t\vec{D} + (C_{p} - C)\} = r^{2}\label{equation:distributivedot1} \\
    (t\vec{D} \cdot t\vec{D}) + \{t\vec{D} \cdot (C_{p} - C)\} + \{t\vec{D} \cdot (C_{p} - C)\} + \{(C_{p} - C) \cdot (C_{p} - C)\} = r^{2}\label{equation:distributivedot2} \\
    (\vec{D} \cdot \vec{D})t^{2} + 2\{\vec{D} \cdot (C_{p} - C)\}t + (C_{p} - C) \cdot (C_{p} - C) - r^{2} = 0\label{equation:textraction}
\end{align}

Taking advantage of the fact that the distance between two points is essentially the magnitude of the vector from one point to the other, the $distance$ function can then be expanded to the dot product of $\{(C_{p} + t\vec{D}) - C\}$ with itself (\ref{equation:dotproductexpansion}). Using the distributive property of dot products, the equation can then futher be expanded to (\ref{equation:distributivedot1}) and (\ref{equation:distributivedot2}). Variable $t$ can be extracted from the dot product to form a quadratic equation (\ref{equation:textraction}). To check that a value of $t$ exists such that the equation holds, the discriminant can be applied to the equation.
\[
{[2\{\vec{D} \cdot (C_{p} - C)\}]}^{2} - 4(\vec{D} \cdot \vec{D})\{(C_{p} - C) \cdot (C_{p} - C) - r^{2}\} \geq 0
\]

\subsection{Camera Movement}
Our implementation allows us to render a sphere, but the calculations presume a viewport location at $(0, 0, 1)$. We will further refine our program to work for any viewport position.

The position of the viewport is always a fixed distance away from the camera. As such, two angles are used to define all possible positions of the viewport, azimuth and elevation.

Afterwards, adjusting our functions to account for a dynamic camera-to-viewport-center vector makes facing different directions with the camera possible.

void addAzimuth(double amount) {
    m_xzTheta += amount; // base of the right triangle with a hypotenuse of m_viewportDistanceFromCamera and angle yzTheta    
    double yTriangleBase = m_viewportDistanceFromCamera * cos(m_yzTheta.radians());
    m_cameraToViewportCenter.x = yTriangleBase * cos(m_xzTheta.radians());
    m_cameraToViewportCenter.z = yTriangleBase * sin(m_xzTheta.radians());
}
void addElevation(double amount) {
    m_yzTheta += amount;

    if (m_yzTheta.get() > 90 and m_yzTheta.get() < 180) {m_yzTheta.set(90);}
    if (m_yzTheta.get() > 180 and m_yzTheta.get() < 270) {m_yzTheta.set(270);} // limit the range of the elevation (it doesn't make sense to be able to look up and then backwards)

    m_cameraToViewportCenter.y = m_viewportDistanceFromCamera * sin(m_yzTheta.radians());
    double yTriangleBase = m_viewportDistanceFromCamera * cos(m_yzTheta.radians()); // base of the right triangle with a hypotenuse of m_viewportDistanceFromCamera and angle yzTheta
    m_cameraToViewportCenter.x = yTriangleBase * cos(m_xzTheta.radians());
    m_cameraToViewportCenter.z = yTriangleBase * sin(m_xzTheta.radians());
}
\end{minted}
\end{code}

It can be noted that the \mintinline{cpp}|xVector| is orthogonal to the camera-to-viewport-center vector and the $y$ axis, and the \mintinline{cpp}|yVector| is orthogonal to the \mintinline{cpp}|xVector| and the camera-to-viewport-center vector.

\begin{code}
\begin{minted}[breaklines]{cpp}
inline constexpr Line getPixelLine(int pixelX, int pixelY, const Custom::Camera& camera) {

    double xDistanceFromCenter {camera.viewportWidth()/camera.screenWidth() *
                                -(pixelX - camera.screenWidth()/2)};
    double yDistanceFromCenter {camera.viewportHeight()/camera.screenHeight() *
                                -(pixelY - camera.screenHeight()/2)};
    
    Vector cameraToViewportCenter {camera.cameraToViewportCenter()};
    Vector xVector {crossProduct(cameraToViewportCenter, {0, -1, 0})};
    Vector yVector {crossProduct(cameraToViewportCenter, xVector)};
    
    double xMultiple {xDistanceFromCenter/sqrt(dotProduct(xVector, xVector))};
    double yMultiple {yDistanceFromCenter/sqrt(dotProduct(yVector, yVector))};

    return {camera.pos(), camera.cameraToViewportCenter() + xMultiple * xVector + yMultiple * yVector};
} // get vector from origin to pixel's equivalent location on viewport
\end{minted}
\end{code}

Although ray tracing is useful in allowing us to represent 3-dimensional objects in a mathematically accurate manner, it comes at the cost of scalability and performance. It is easy enough to ray trace mathematically simple objects such as spheres (as demonstrated); however, attempting to ray trace objects other than spheres proves to be more difficult. Although not impossible, as it is possible to approximate 3D shapes using  2D polygons and trace each polygon separately, the core obstacle lies in performance: it is simply too costly to compute every single ray-object interaction for every pixel. Therefore, a different method, one optimized for drawing polygons that is more performant at the cost of quality and accuracy, is needed for applications that require high framerates.

\section{Rasterization}
\subsection{Polygon Projection}
To implement our rasterizer, code from the raytracer will be reused where applicable. This includes the \mintinline{cpp}|Degree|, \mintinline{cpp}|Camera|, \mintinline{cpp}|Point|, and \mintinline{cpp}|Vector| classes.

Rasterization is a technique that involves approximating any 3D object into a collection of 2D polygons, commonly triangles. Triangles are commonly used as they are the simplest $n$-sided polygon, which every other polygon can be split into.

\begin{figure}[htp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[scale = 0.5]{Dolphin_triangle_mesh.png}
    \caption{A 3D model approximated into triangles}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htp]
    \centering
    \centering
    \includesvg[scale = 1.5]{Polygon Triangulation.svg}
    \caption{A demonstration of polygon triangulation}
\end{figure}

Approximating models into triangles is convenient, as all we have to figure out is how to properly display a triangle in 3D space onto a 2D plane, and repeat that process for every single triangle in the scene.

The next step is to determine a method to render a triangle suspended in 3D space onto a 2D plane. Focusing on a single point on the triangle $P$, and assuming a viewport center position of $(0, 0, 1)$ for simplicity, a side view of the point in relation to the viewport reveals 2 triangles, $\triangle OPA$ and $\triangle OP'A'$.

\begin{figure}
    \includesvg{PP.svg}\caption{A side ($yz$ plane) view of an arbitrary point $P$}
\end{figure}

As with ray tracing, we take advantage of the fact that light travels in a straight line, meaning that $P'$ is where we must draw $P$ on the viewport. Thus, the task at hand is to calculate the position of $P'$ of an arbitrary point $P$. It can be noted that point $A$ is designated so that $\angle PAO$ is a right angle. Therefore, $\triangle OPA \sim \triangle OP'A'$, and as such, $\overline{A'P'} : \overline{AP} = \overline{OP'} : \overline{OP} = \overline{OA'} : \overline{OA}$. The coordinates of point $P$ are given, point $A$ is able to derived from point $P$ by taking its $z$ coordinate, and point $A'$ and the $z$ coordinate of $P'$ are constants. Therfore, the only variable, the $y$ coordinate of point $P$, is able to be derived:

\[
    P'_{y} = \frac{\overline{PA}}{\overline{OA}} \cdot \overline{OA'} = \frac{P_{y}}{P_{z}} \cdot 1
\]

$P'_{x}$ is able to be derived in a similar fashion, except this time from the view of the $xy$ plane.

\[
    P'_{x} = \frac{P_{x}}{P_{z}} \cdot 1
\]

We can convert the world space coordinates of $P'$ into screen space coordinate with the formula below (where $V_{w}$ and $V_{h}$ refer to viewport width and height respectively, while $S_{w}$ and $S_{h}$ refer to screen width and height respectively):

\[
    P'_{cx} = -\frac{P'_{x}}{V_{w}} \cdot S_{w} + \frac{S_{w}}{2}
\]
\[
    P'_{cy} = -\frac{P'_{y}}{V_{h}} \cdot S_{h} + \frac{S_h}{2}
\]

Now that we have a method to obtain the $P'$ coordinate of every point on the triangle $P$, all that is left is to fill in the rest of the triangle onto the screen. It can be noted that straight lines in 3D space remain straight even when projected onto a 2D surface; therefore, connecting the three points drawn onto the screen results in the correctly projected triangle.

\begin{figure}[htp]
    \centering
    \includesvg[scale=0.75]{projection.svg}
    \caption{Projection of a 3D triangle onto a 2D viewport}
\end{figure}

To draw a triangle, we must first figure out how to connect two screen space coordinates. The equation of a line passing through two points $A$ and $B$ is as follows:
\[
    y - A_{y} = \frac{B_{y} - A_{y}}{B_{x} - A_{x}} (x - A_{x})
\]
Computer screens approximate images with pixels, so drawing a mathematically accurate line is not possible. However, an approximation is possible by rounding to the nearest integer. For every $x$ coordinate of a pixel on the screen, the pixel's $y$ coordinate can be derived by plugging the value of $x$ into our formula for the line. After that, the y value is rounded to the nearest integer to color in the pixel closest to the given $x$ and $y$ coordinates. Therfore, to draw a line between two pixel coordinates, a \mintinline{cpp}|for| loop that iterates over every integer $x$ value between the two points is used. Every iteration, the $y$ coordinate for that pixel is derived, and the pixel is colored in. An optimization can be made here by adding the slope of the line to the $y$ coordinate every iteration instead of recalculating, as multiplication is simply repeated addition.

\begin{minted}[breaklines]{cpp}
double slope {(y2 - y1) / static_cast<double>((x2 - x1))};
double y {static_cast<double>(x1)};

for (int x {x1}; x <= x2; ++x) {
    DrawPixel(x, y);
    y += slope;
}
\end{minted}

However, a problem occurs when the slope of the line is greater than $1$. Since we are drawing one pixel for every $x$ coordinate, the algorithm results in disconnected lines (Figure~\ref{figure:disconnectedlines}).
\begin{figure}[htp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[scale=0.5]{Disconnected Lines.jpg}\caption{Disconnected lines}\label{figure:disconnectedlines}
\end{figure}
This happens because screen spaces are integer approximations of real numbers, and since the slope is greater than 1, for every 1 pixel difference in $x$, the $y$ coordinate increases by more than 1, leaving gaps. As such, for lines with a slope greater than 1, instead of drawing 1 pixel for every $x$ coordinate, we need to draw 1 pixel for every $y$ coordinate, filling in the gaps. To accomplish this, instead of iterating over every $x$ value between the two points, we instead iterate over every $y$ value between the two points and derive the $x$ coordinate from the $y$ values instead. This ensures that the drawn line remains connected.

This process can be generalized by designating an independent variable $i$, the variable we choose values for, and a dependent variable $d$, the variable whose values depend on $i$. In our \mintinline{cpp}|for| loop, we iterate over every integer $i$ value and derive its appropriate $d$ value. This is known as a linear interpolation function, a function that returns a line connecting two points. A linear interpolation function that returns an array of the dependent coordinates of a line connecting two points, $(i1, d1)$, $(i2, d2)$, in 2D space can be implemented as such:
\begin{code}
\begin{minted}[breaklines]{cpp}
std::vector<double> interpolate(int i1, int d1, int i2, int d2) {
    if (i1 == i2) {
        return {static_cast<double>(d1)};
    }

    if (i2 < i1) {
        std::swap(i1, i2);
        std::swap(d1, d2);
    }

    std::vector<double> retArray (i2 - i1 + 1);
    double slope {(d2 - d1) / static_cast<double>((i2 - i1))};
    double d {static_cast<double>(d1)};

    for (int i {i1}; i <= i2; ++i) {
        retArray[i - i1] = d;
        d += slope;
    }
    
    return retArray;
}
\end{minted}
\end{code}
For a point $(i, d)$ on the line, \mintinline{cpp}|retarray|[$i$] = $d$. For cases where the two points are the same, we simply return an array of a single point $d1$.

This generalization of the interpolating process is useful as it will serve as the foundation of our rasterizer.

\begin{code}
\begin{minted}[breaklines]{cpp}
void drawLine(int x1, int y1, int x2, int y2, raylib::Color color = BLACK) {
    std::vector<double> lineArray {};

    if (x2 - x1 > y2 - y1) {
        lineArray = interpolate(x1, y1, x2, y2);

        for (int x {x1}; x <= x2; ++x) {
            DrawPixel(x, lineArray[x - x1], color);
        }
    }
    else {
        lineArray = interpolate(y1, x1, y2, x2);

        for (int y {y1}; y <= y2; ++y) {
            DrawPixel(lineArray[y - y1], y, color);
        }
    }
}   
\end{minted}
\end{code}

When the slope is less than 1, the linear interpolation function is called with $x$ as the independent variable ($i$) and $y$ as the dependent variable ($d$). When the slope is greater than or equal to 1, the function is instead called with $y$ as the independent variable and $x$ as the dependent variable.

With our \mintinline{cpp}|drawLine()| function and linear interpolation function, we can now draw 3D triangles onto a 2D screen. Now all that remains is to fill in the triangle.

We can go about accomplishing this in many ways, but one of the most straightforward methods is to imagine the process as painting in a horizontal strip of the triangle from the left edge all the way to the right edge, then working your way down and repeating until everything is painted in.

\begin{figure}[htp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[scale=0.4]{Triangle Fill.png}\autocite{TriangleFill}
\end{figure}

By sorting the 3 vertices of the triangle in terms of their $y$ coordinates in descending order, we can designate two `edges' that will serve as the left and right borders for our horizontal strips. One edge is the geometric edge between the highest point and the lowest point. The other edge is the combination of two geometric edges, the edge connecting the highest point with the middle point and the edge connecting the middle point with the lowest point. Which edge becomes the left edge and which edge becomes the right edge depends on the configuration of the points (Figure~\ref{Figure:trianglevertices}).

\begin{figure}[htp]
    \centering
    \includesvg[scale=0.95]{triangleEdges.svg}\caption{}\label{Figure:trianglevertices}
\end{figure}

Our linear interpolation function comes in handy here, as it can be used to return arrays of every point along the necessary edges. For both edges, we must use the linear interpolation function with $y$ as the independent variable, as we need to be able to color in every single $y$ `level' in the triangle. Once we obtain our left and right edges, we can iterate through both arrays simultaneously, and every operation, we color in the every pixel in between the left and right edges at the current $y$ level.

\begin{code}
\begin{minted}[breaklines]{cpp}
void draw() const {
    std::vector<double> x123 {interpolate(m_p1.y, m_p1.x, m_p2.y, m_p2.x)};
    x123.pop_back();
    std::vector<double> x23 {interpolate(m_p2.y, m_p2.x, m_p3.y, m_p3.x)};
    x123.insert(x123.end(), x23.begin(), x23.end());
    std::vector<double> x31 {interpolate(m_p3.y, m_p3.x, m_p1.y, m_p1.x)};
    
    assert(x123.size() == x31.size());

    std::vector<double> x_left {};
    std::vector<double> x_right {};
    if (x31[x31.size() / 2] < x123[x123.size() / 2]) {
        x_left = x31;
        x_right = x123;
    }
    else {
        x_left = x123;
        x_right = x31;
    }
    
    for (int y {m_p1.y}; y <= m_p3.y; ++y) {
        for (int x {static_cast<int>(std::ceil(x_left[y - m_p1.y]))}; x <= x_right[y - m_p1.y]; ++x) {
            DrawPixel(x, y, m_color);
        }
    }
}
\end{minted}
\end{code}

\subsection{Model Transformation}

Let's say we would like to illustrate a scene, an ocean full of dolphins. In this case, we need to be able to render multiple dolphins in the scene, all with their own distinct positions, orientations, and sizes. This calls for the need to separately designate \textbf{models} and \textbf{model instances}. A model is an object containing all the information about its vertices and which ones connect to form triangles, and a model instance is an object that contains a model object, with additional member variables denoting its rotation, scale, and position. This also means that models exist in their own \textbf{model space} before being transformed into its world space coordinates depending on a model instance's member variables.

We first define a primitive version of our model and model instance class objects:
\begin{code}
    \begin{minted}{cpp}
namespace Custom {
    struct Model {
        std::vector<Point3> vertices {};
        std::vector<std::array<int, 3>> triangles {{}};
        std::vector<raylib::Color> colors {triangles.size()};
        Point3 center {ORIGIN};
    };

    class ModelInstance {
        public:
            ModelInstance(Model model, Point3 position, double scale = 1) 
                : m_model {model}, m_position {position}, m_scale {scale}
            {}

            void draw(Custom::Camera& camera) const;

            void addRoll(double amount);
            void addPitch(double amount);
            void addYaw(double amount);

        private:
            Model m_model {};
            Point3 m_position {0, 0, 0};
            double m_scale {1};
    };
}
    \end{minted}
\end{code}

A primitive \mintinline{cpp}|draw()| function can be implemented as such:

\begin{code}
    \begin{minted}[breaklines]{cpp}
void Custom::ModelInstance::draw(Custom::Camera& camera) const {

    std::vector<ScreenPoint2> pointsOnScreen {};
    pointsOnScreen.reserve(m_model.vertices.size());
    for (auto a: m_model.vertices) {
        Vector deltas {m_position - m_model.center};
        double px {a.x/a.z};
        double py {a.y/a.z};

        int cx {static_cast<int>(std::round(px/camera.viewportWidth() * camera.screenWidth()))};
        int cy {static_cast<int>(std::round(py/camera.viewportHeight() * camera.screenHeight()))};
        pointsOnScreen.push_back({-cx + camera.screenWidth()/2, -cy + camera.screenHeight()/2});
    }
    int count {0};
    for (auto a: m_model.triangles) {
        Triangle{{pointsOnScreen[a[0]]}, {pointsOnScreen[a[1]]}, {pointsOnScreen[a[2]]}, m_model.colors[count]}.draw();
        ++count;
    }
}
    \end{minted}
\end{code}

Now, we must implement a \mintinline{cpp}|transform()| function that can apply the 3 transformations of the model instance: rotation, then scaling, then translation in that order. In order to achieve this, a closer examination of vector and space properties is required. For the sake of simplicity and ease of understanding, the following vector concepts will be explained in terms of 2 dimensions. These concepts can then be easily expanded to apply for 3 dimensions.

A vector is composed of scalar multiples of the unit vectors. A \textbf{unit vector} is a vector with length 1 that only travels along one axis. In 2 dimensions, the unit vectors are:
\[
\ihat = 
\begin{bmatrix}
    1 \\
    0
\end{bmatrix}
\jhat = 
\begin{bmatrix}
    0 \\
    1
\end{bmatrix}
\]
\begin{figure}[htp]
    \centering
    \includesvg{vectorUnits.svg}\caption{The vector $2\ihat + 1\jhat$}
\end{figure}
Using the 2 unit vectors $\ihat$ and $\jhat$, every possible vector can be expressed. Extending the concept, we can say that space itself is defined with a combination of scalar multiples of the unit vectors serving as the basis; in other words, every possible point in space can be defined as a vector made up of scalar multiples of the unit vectors. This works because points and vectors are similar to the point of being nearly interchangeable. Any point can be swapped out for a vector going from the origin point to that point, and any computations involving that point also work with its equivalent vector; even the contents of their notations are conveniently similar. Going back to our use case, we can take advantage of the fact that transforming the unit vectors composing a vector also applies that same transformation to the vector itself (Figure~\ref{figure:vectorTransformation}), then apply this for the points on our model.

\begin{figure}[htp]
    \centering
    \includesvg[scale=0.9]{vectorTransformation.svg}\caption{The angle between $\vec{V}$ and $\vec{V\prime}$ is equal to the angle between $\ihat$ and $\ihat\prime$}\label{figure:vectorTransformation}
\end{figure}

As such, we can store the information of the transformed unit vectors using a matrix. A \textbf{matrix} is a rectangular array of numbers, composed of rows and columns, useful for a variety of mathematical applications. In our case, we can use matrices to perform coordinate transformations.

We store the information of the transformed unit vectors into a matrix like so:

\[
\ihat^\prime = 
\begin{bmatrix}
    \ihat^\prime_x \\
    \ihat^\prime_y
\end{bmatrix}
\jhat^\prime = 
\begin{bmatrix}
    \jhat^\prime_x \\
    \jhat^\prime_y
\end{bmatrix}\rightarrow 
M_t =
\begin{bmatrix}
    \ihat^\prime_x & \jhat^\prime_x\\
    \ihat^\prime_y & \jhat^\prime_y
\end{bmatrix}
\]
Where $\ihat^\prime$ and $\jhat^\prime$ are the transformed unit vectors, and $M_t$ is the transformation matrix. It can also be observed that matrices have similar notation to vectors, and this is not a coincidence: matrices can be thought of as an extension of vectors (they are simply storing multiple vectors), and vectors can be thought of as matrices with only 1 column.

Now that we have our transformation matrix, we can multiply it with a vector to apply the transformation. In simple terms, a vector notated as the set of its components $\vec{V} = \left\langle x, y\right\rangle $ tells us how much of each unit vector makes up the vector. For example, the vector $\left\langle 2, 1\right\rangle $ is is the sum of $2\ihat$ and $1\jhat$.

Our matrix stores the information of the transformed unit vectors, so we can obtain the transformed vector by multiplying the transformed unit vectors by the scalar components of the original vector. In other words, the transformed vector, instead of being composed of scalar multiples of the unit vectors $\ihat$ and $\jhat$, is now composed of scalar multiples of $\ihat^\prime$ and $\jhat^\prime$ (Figure~\ref{figure:vectorTransformation}).
\[
\vec{V} =
\begin{bmatrix}
    a \\
    b
\end{bmatrix}
\]
\[
M_t = 
\begin{bmatrix}
    \ihat^\prime_x & \jhat^\prime_x\\
    \ihat^\prime_y & \jhat^\prime_y
\end{bmatrix}
\]
\[
\vec{V^\prime} = M_t \times \vec{V} =
\begin{bmatrix}
    a\ihat^\prime_x + b\jhat^\prime_x \\
    a\ihat^\prime_y + b\jhat^\prime_y
\end{bmatrix}
\]
It is also possible to multiply a matrix by another matrix, resulting in a matrix that combines the two transformations:
\[
\begin{bmatrix}
    a & c \\
    b & d \\
\end{bmatrix}
\times
\begin{bmatrix}
    e & g \\
    f & h
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
    ea + cf & ag + ch \\
    eb + df & bg + dh
\end{bmatrix}
\]
The resulting matrix describes the transformation of multiplying with the left matrix, then the right matrix. Note that transformations are not commutative ($M_a \times M_b \neq M_b \times M_a$).

Matrices are a much more complex topic and foundational to the field of linear algebra, but matrix concepts outside those relevant to our use case are outside the scope of this paper. As such, this paper will be adhering to only explaining and laying the groundwork for matrix operations useful for our rasterizer.

Now that we have a method to transform vectors around a point (in the above examples, the origin point $O$), we can begin to implement functions to transform our model. First, we define a \mintinline{cpp}|matrix| class:

\begin{code}
    \begin{minted}[breaklines]{cpp}
namespace Custom {
    class Matrix {
        public:
            Matrix(size_t rows, size_t cols, const std::vector<double>& data = {})
                : m_rows {rows}, m_cols {cols}, m_data (rows * cols)
            {
                if (data.size() != 0) {m_data = data;}
            }

            double& operator()(size_t row, size_t col);
            double operator()(size_t row, size_t col) const;
            Matrix getColumn(size_t col) const;
            Matrix getRow(size_t row) const;
            void setColumn(size_t col, Matrix colMatrix);
            operator Point3() const;

            friend constexpr Matrix operator*(const Matrix&, const Matrix&);
            friend constexpr Matrix operator*(double scalar, const Matrix&);
            friend constexpr Matrix operator+(const Matrix&, const Matrix&);
            friend constexpr Matrix& operator+=(Matrix&, const Matrix&);

            
        private:
            size_t m_rows {1};
            size_t m_cols {1};
            std::vector<double> m_data {};
    };

    constexpr Matrix operator+(const Matrix& rMatrix, const Matrix& lMatrix) {
        assert(rMatrix.m_rows == lMatrix.m_rows and rMatrix.m_cols == lMatrix.m_cols);
        Matrix retMatrix {rMatrix.m_rows, rMatrix.m_cols};

        for (size_t row {0}; row < rMatrix.m_rows; ++row) {
            for (size_t col {0}; col < rMatrix.m_cols; ++col) {
                retMatrix(row, col) = rMatrix(row, col) + lMatrix(row, col);
            }
        }

        return retMatrix;
    }

    constexpr Matrix& operator+=(Matrix& lMatrix, const Matrix& rMatrix) {
        lMatrix = lMatrix + rMatrix;
        return lMatrix;
    }


    constexpr Matrix operator*(const Matrix& lMatrix, const Matrix& rMatrix) {
        assert(lMatrix.m_cols == rMatrix.m_rows);
        Matrix retMatrix {lMatrix.m_rows, rMatrix.m_cols};
        for (size_t rcol {0}; rcol < rMatrix.m_cols; ++rcol) {
            Matrix currColumn {lMatrix.m_rows, 1};
            for (size_t lrow {0}; lrow < lMatrix.m_rows; ++lrow) {
                currColumn += rMatrix(lrow, rcol) * lMatrix.getColumn(lrow);
            }
            retMatrix.setColumn(rcol, currColumn);
        }
        return retMatrix;
    };

    constexpr Matrix operator*(double scalar, const Matrix& matrix) {
        Matrix retMatrix {matrix.m_rows, matrix.m_cols};
        for (size_t col {0}; col < matrix.m_cols; ++col) {
            for (size_t row {0}; row < matrix.m_rows; ++row) {
                retMatrix(row, col) = matrix(row, col) * scalar;
            }
        }
        return retMatrix;
    }
}
    \end{minted}
\end{code}

We can now add an \mintinline{cpp}|m_transform| matrix variable.
\begin{code}
    \begin{minted}[breaklines]{cpp}
Custom::Matrix m_transform {m_scale * (m_rollTransform * m_pitchTransform * m_yawTransform)};
    \end{minted}
\end{code}

For convenience, all our \mintinline{cpp}|model| objects will have their center coordinates at the origin point of their model space, meaning any rotations will happen around the origin point of their model space. This streamlines the rotation process and allows us to avoid additional complexity in defining our rotation matrices.

To briefly summarize, the process of transforming a \mintinline{cpp}|model| object's coordinates into its \mintinline{cpp}|modelInstance| world space coordinates is as follows: we iterate through every single point in the model, applying a transformation matrix that is the rotation and scale matrices multiplied, then apply the translation by adding the value of its position coordinates.

This calls for the need to add a new matrix member variable \mintinline{cpp}|m_transform| in the \mintinline{cpp}|modelInstance| class.
\begin{code}
    \begin{minted}{cpp}
Custom::Matrix m_transform {3, 3, {
    m_scale, 0, 0,
    0, m_scale, 0,
    0, 0, m_scale
}};
    \end{minted}
\end{code}
In 3 dimensions, 3 angles, known as Euler Angles, are needed to define every possible orientation of an object in 3D space. This is because there are 3 different combinations of the $x, y$ and $z$ axes that make a 2D plane. In 2 dimensions, as there is only 1 possible combination of the $x$ and $y$ axes, only 1 angle is needed.

Rotation matrices for rotations around each of the axes are as follows:
\[
{\displaystyle {\begin{alignedat}{1}R_{x}(\theta )&={\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&\cos \theta &-\sin \theta \\[3pt]0&\sin \theta &\cos \theta \\[3pt]\end{bmatrix}}\\[6pt]R_{y}(\theta )&={\begin{bmatrix}\cos \theta &0&\sin \theta \\[3pt]0&1&0\\[3pt]-\sin \theta &0&\cos \theta \\\end{bmatrix}}\\[6pt]R_{z}(\theta )&={\begin{bmatrix}\cos \theta &-\sin \theta &0\\[3pt]\sin \theta &\cos \theta &0\\[3pt]0&0&1\\\end{bmatrix}}\end{alignedat}}}
\]

As such, in order to be able to achieve every possible orientation in 3D space, an object must be able to rotate in 3 different planes, or in other words, around the 3 axes. Therefore, our rasterizer program will imitate the object rotation mechanism used in the open source 3D software Blender (Figure~\ref{figure:blenderPreview}).

\begin{figure}
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{Blender Window}\caption{A preview of the rotation mechanism in Blender}\label{figure:blenderPreview}
\end{figure}

We can achieve this by implementing three new functions:
\begin{code}
    \begin{minted}[breaklines]{cpp}
void Custom::ModelInstance::rotateX(double amount) {
    Custom::Matrix rollTransform = {3, 3, {
    1, 0, 0, 
    0, std::cos(Degree{amount}.radians()), -std::sin(Degree{amount}.radians()),
    0, std::sin(Degree{amount}.radians()), std::cos(Degree{amount}.radians())}
    };
    m_transform = rollTransform * m_transform;
}
void Custom::ModelInstance::rotateY(double amount) {
    Custom::Matrix pitchTransform = {3, 3, {
    std::cos(Degree{amount}.radians()), 0, std::sin(Degree{amount}.radians()), 
    0, 1, 0,
    -std::sin(Degree{amount}.radians()), 0, std::cos(Degree{amount}.radians())}
    };
    m_transform = pitchTransform * m_transform;
}
void Custom::ModelInstance::rotateZ(double amount) {
    Custom::Matrix yawTransform = {3, 3, {
    std::cos(Degree{amount}.radians()), -std::sin(Degree{amount}.radians()), 0, 
    std::sin(Degree{amount}.radians()), std::cos(Degree{amount}.radians()), 0,
    0, 0, 1}
    };
    m_transform = yawTransform * m_transform;
}
    \end{minted}
\end{code}
We then create a \mintinline{cpp}|transform| function that takes in a point as input and multiplies it by the \mintinline{cpp}|m_transform| matrix (treating the point as a vector from the origin to that point), and then also applies the translation afterwards.
\begin{code}
    \begin{minted}[breaklines]{cpp}
Point3& Custom::ModelInstance::transform(Point3& modelPoint) const {
    modelPoint = m_transform * modelPoint;
    
    modelPoint.x += m_position.x - m_model.center.x;
    modelPoint.y += m_position.y - m_model.center.y;
    modelPoint.z += m_position.z - m_model.center.z;
        
    return modelPoint;
}
    \end{minted}
\end{code}
We then add a line of code to our \mintinline{cpp}|draw()| function inside the first \mintinline{cpp}|for| loop to transform every single point in the model.
\begin{code}
    \begin{minted}{cpp}
a = transform(a);
    \end{minted}
\end{code}

Lastly, just like with the raytracer, we need to implement camera movement. It should be noted that when the camera's viewport rotates by a certain angle, from the perspective of the camera, the object rotates around the camera in the opposite direction by that angle amount instead. Imitating this, instead of moving the camera in the world space like with the raytracer, we instead define a camera space where the camera is always at $(0, 0, 0)$ and the viewport is always centered at $(0, 0, 1)$. After that, we calculate how much the camera's coordinates and the viewport's orientation in relation to the camera in the world space deviate from their configurations in camera space. Then, we apply the reverse of that deviation onto every object before placing them into our camera space to be displayed on the screen.

Although this sounds complicated, in reality, this is as simple as adding a single line of code. If we assume the camera never moves from the origin point for the sake of simplicity, we can add the following line in our \mintinline{cpp}|transform()| function after the rotation and translation happen to ensure that camera movement is applied correctly.
\begin{code}
    \begin{minted}[breaklines]{cpp}
modelPoint = camera.m_azimuthTransform * (camera.m_elevationTransform * modelPoint);
    \end{minted}
\end{code}
It should be noted that the object is rotated by the elevation angle before the azimuth angle, due to how our viewport angles are configured.
